What drew me most to Hito Steyerl’s essay was the ambivalent status of the poor image. From one perspective, the poor image can be a form of resistance to the hierarchy of high resolution. It does not depend on professional equipment or institutional distribution. It can circulate outside museums, cinemas, archives and other established institutions. This allows marginalized works to reach a wider audience. In this way, the poor image challenges the idea that only polished and high-resolution images have artistic value. At the same time, what makes the poor image resistant also makes it useful to information capitalism. Users may see circulation as participation or freedom. However, platforms can turn that activity into engagement, data and profit. This raises a question for me: How can we know if an image reaches us freely or if a platform, company or political institution delivers it to us for a specific reason? Algorithms already affect what becomes visible and what disappears. Even when we like, repost or comment on something, we are choosing from content that has already been selected for us. How can we become active participants instead of passive consumers of images? This contradiction also made me think about the control of images and films in China. Political control is different from capitalist exploitation, but both make me question who decides what we can see. When films or historical images are restricted, people may still share low-quality copies through screenshots, private messages or foreign platforms. Poor images can help restricted information survive. However, they may also lose their original context and become easier to misunderstand or manipulate. This reading makes me rethink how I do not need to see imperfect images as unfinished or less valuable; degradation could be a new expression in a way. Instead, I want to pay more attention to where I share my work, how platforms may change its meaning, and whether my work invites people to think rather than only consume. My main takeaway is that the poor image is neither fully liberating nor fully oppressive. Its impact depends on who shares it, who controls it, and why it is being circulated.
